What Does It Cost to Sell a Luxury Home in Arizona? Costs, Taxes & Pre-Marketing

By Laura Jorden, Compass  ·  Published November 16, 2026

What does it cost to sell a luxury home in Arizona?

Selling a luxury home in Arizona involves five main cost groups: your listing brokerage's compensation, which is negotiable; any buyer-agent compensation or concessions you choose to offer; escrow and title charges; HOA disclosure and transfer fees; and prorated property taxes. Arizona has no real estate transfer tax, so most of the big numbers are ones you negotiate.

On top of those, plan for your loan or line-of-credit payoff, any pre-listing repairs, staging, and moving. Because most of these items are negotiated or depend on your specific community, I prefer to give sellers a written net sheet built from their own numbers rather than quote a rule-of-thumb percentage. Escrow fees are typically split between buyer and seller, and who pays for the owner's title insurance policy is a commonly negotiated item in Arizona contracts (First American).

Does Arizona charge a transfer tax when you sell a home?

No. Article IX, Section 24 of the Arizona Constitution bars the state, counties, cities, and taxing districts from imposing any new tax, fee, or stamp requirement on the sale or transfer of real property. Charges that already existed on December 31, 2007, such as routine recording fees, still apply, but there is no percentage-of-price transfer tax.

For sellers on a multimillion-dollar home, that is a real difference from many other states, and it is one reason Arizona's seller costs are driven mostly by negotiation rather than by government charges.

Who pays real estate commissions when you sell in Arizona?

You negotiate your listing brokerage's compensation directly; according to the National Association of REALTORS®, it "remains fully negotiable." Since August 17, 2024, offers of compensation to buyers' agents can't appear in the MLS. You can still choose to offer buyer-agent compensation or closing-cost concessions, with the terms disclosed and approved in writing.

In practice, many buyers of luxury homes now negotiate their agent's compensation as part of the offer. Deciding in advance whether you'll offer buyer-agent compensation, concessions, or neither is one of the most important pricing conversations Laura Jorden has with sellers, because it affects both your net and the size of your buyer pool.

What HOA fees does a seller pay at closing in Arizona?

For planned communities, A.R.S. 33-1806 caps the association's resale disclosure fee at $400 in total, plus up to $100 for rush service within 72 hours and up to $50 for an update requested 30 or more days later. Condominiums follow the same caps under A.R.S. 33-1260. Any separate transfer fee comes from the community's declaration.

The statute also says these fees can't be collected before close of escrow and can be charged only once per transaction. Some communities set a separate title transfer or capital improvement fee in their declaration, and those are not capped by this section. I pull the community documents early so the full amount is on your net sheet before you list.

How are property taxes handled when you sell in Maricopa County?

Arizona property taxes are billed in two installments, according to the Arizona Department of Revenue. The first is due October 1 and delinquent after November 1; the second is due March 1 of the following year and delinquent after May 1. At closing, escrow prorates the year's taxes between you and the buyer.

If you are selling between now and spring, it's worth checking whether the first-half bill has been paid, since that changes whether the proration shows up as a credit to you or a charge on your statement.

Will I owe capital gains tax when I sell my Scottsdale home?

Possibly, if your gain is large. The IRS lets you exclude up to $250,000 of gain on the sale of your main home, or up to $500,000 on a joint return, if you owned and lived in it for at least two of the five years before the sale. Long-time luxury owners can exceed those limits.

Your CPA can tell you how improvements you've made over the years affect your gain, and how a second home or investment property is treated differently. It's a conversation to have before you set a list price, not after you accept an offer.

What is pre-marketing before the MLS, and is it allowed?

Pre-marketing means letting qualified buyers and agents know about your home before it appears on public home-search sites. It's allowed within limits. Under the Clear Cooperation Policy that ARMLS has adopted, a listing must be entered into the MLS within one business day of any public marketing, such as a yard sign, email blast, or website post.

Within those rules, Arizona sellers generally have three ways to launch quietly:

  • Office exclusive. The home is marketed only within the listing brokerage, with no public advertising. If it is advertised publicly, it must go into the MLS.
  • ARMLS Coming Soon. The home can sit in Coming Soon for up to 30 days. It is visible to all MLS agents but is not syndicated to public sites, and days on market don't start until it goes Active (ARMLS).
  • Delayed marketing. Under NAR's Multiple Listing Options for Sellers policy from March 2025, a seller can delay internet display and syndication for a period each MLS sets, after signing a disclosure about the benefits of immediate public marketing.

At Compass, sellers can also use a phased launch that moves from a private exclusive to Coming Soon and then to the full MLS (Real Estate News).

Is pre-marketing right for my luxury home?

It depends on what you value most. Pre-marketing can protect your privacy, let you test a price with serious buyers, and keep days on market at zero while you gather feedback. The trade-off is a smaller audience. Held too long, a quiet launch can leave money on the table, because broad exposure is what creates competing offers.

Portal rules matter too. Zillow's Listing Access Standards, announced in April 2025, can keep off Zillow a listing that was publicly marketed without being widely available through the MLS, and Zillow said in March 2026 that those standards remain in effect while it launched a Preview feature for pre-market listings (Real Estate News). Laura Jorden walks each seller through where their home will and won't appear under each option, so the choice is yours with the facts in front of you.

How can I estimate my net proceeds before I list?

Ask for a written net sheet. I build one for each seller using a realistic sale price range, your actual loan payoff, the HOA's disclosure and transfer fees, the current tax proration, and the compensation and concessions you choose to offer. Seeing those numbers side by side lets you set price and launch strategy with confidence.

Common Questions

Is there a real estate transfer tax in Arizona?

No. Article IX, Section 24 of the Arizona Constitution bars the state and local governments from imposing any new tax or fee on the sale or transfer of real property. Charges that already existed on December 31, 2007, such as routine recording fees, still apply.

Are real estate commissions negotiable in Arizona?

Yes. Compensation for your listing brokerage is fully negotiable. Since August 17, 2024, offers of compensation to buyers' agents cannot be published in the MLS, but sellers may still offer buyer-agent compensation or closing-cost concessions with written disclosure and approval.

How much can an Arizona HOA charge a seller for resale documents?

Under A.R.S. 33-1806 for planned communities and 33-1260 for condominiums, up to $400 in total for the resale disclosure, plus up to $100 for rush service within 72 hours and up to $50 for an update. Transfer fees set in a community's declaration are separate.

How long can a home stay in Coming Soon status in ARMLS?

Up to 30 days, and no more than 30 of every 45 calendar days. Days on market do not start until the listing goes Active, and Coming Soon listings are visible to MLS agents but are not syndicated to public home-search sites.

Will I owe capital gains tax when I sell my primary home?

Possibly. Federal rules let you exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 on a joint return, if you owned and lived in the home for at least two of the five years before the sale. Gain above that may be taxable, so check with your CPA.
